# **ABSTRACT** 

The proliferation of sophisticated cyber threats has rendered traditional, signature-based Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) increasingly inadequate against zero-day exploits, necessitating a structural shift toward behavioral machine learning (ML) models. However, while contemporary ML models demonstrate high detection accuracy in academic literature, the vast majority are evaluated strictly in static, offline batch-processing environments. This creates a critical real-time deployability gap, ignoring the strict latency constraints, throughput limits, and active visualization requirements necessary for live operational deployment. To address this gap, this study focuses on the design, development, and implementation of a real-time behavioral NIDS engineered to process continuous network traffic streams. Guided by the Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM) and the CRISP-DM framework, the system utilizes the refined IDS2025 benchmark dataset to mitigate severe class imbalance and legacy data artifacts. The study comparatively evaluates three distinct algorithmic paradigms: Isolation Forest for unsupervised anomaly isolation, Random Forest for supervised ensemble classification, and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks for sequential deep learning. To validate operational feasibility, the selected models are deployed within a decoupled, asynchronous producer-consumer architecture that ingests flow records over continuous TCP sockets. The backend inference engine is integrated with an interactive Streamlit web dashboard, providing security administrators with sub-second threat telemetry and visual alert generation. The system is evaluated across two core dimensions: predictive classification accuracy (Precision, Recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC) and system-level performance (end-to-end pipeline latency and flow throughput). Ultimately, this research bridges the divide between theoretical model accuracy and practical network defense, delivering a reproducible, low-latency framework for continuous threat monitoring in resource-constrained environments.

# **CHAPTER ONE**

## **INTRODUCTION**

## **1.1 Background of Study**

The contemporary digital landscape is characterized by an absolute and growing dependence of modern institutions, businesses, and individuals on interconnected digital networks, a trend that is unfortunately matched by an exponential rise in the sophistication, frequency, and severity of cyberattacks. Security threats such as Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks, port scanning, brute-force login attempts, malicious botnets, and sophisticated web-based exploits have transitioned from occasional anomalies to routine daily occurrences on networks of all scales, ranging from corporate data centers to academic networks. This rapid expansion of the attack surface is heavily driven by the integration of emerging fourth industrial revolution technologies, including the Internet of Things (IoT), cloud computing, edge networks, and mobile connectivity (Pinto et al., 2023; Pinto Neto et al., 2025). Consequently, establishing a resilient defensive posture has become a matter of critical national security and organizational survival, as successful security breaches can lead to massive financial losses, systemic operational disruptions, and severe compromises of data privacy (Chua et al., 2024; Pinto et al., 2023).

Within this complex environment, Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) play a fundamental role as a primary defensive mechanism by continuously monitoring network traffic to identify actions that deviate from expected or authorized behaviors. These defensive systems are generally categorized into two main classes based on their deployment scope, specifically Host-based Intrusion Detection Systems (HIDS) and Network-based Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) (Ali et al., 2024; Maseer et al., 2021). Host-based systems are deployed directly on individual endpoints, such as servers or workstations, where they monitor local operating system calls and logs (Ali et al., 2024). In contrast, network-based systems are positioned at strategic chokepoints or perimeters within the network architecture, where they analyze transmitted packets and flow-level statistics across multiple connected devices without requiring individual host agents (Ali et al., 2024; Maseer et al., 2021).

Historically, intrusion detection systems have relied on two primary methodologies for threat identification, which are signature-based and anomaly-based detection (Ali et al., 2024; Maseer et al., 2021). Signature-based systems, also referred to as misuse detection, operate by matching incoming network traffic patterns against a pre-configured database of known attack fingerprints (Ali et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Although signature-based systems are highly effective at identifying well-known, established threats with exceptionally low false-positive rates, they suffer from a severe structural limitation because they are completely incapable of detecting novel or zero-day attacks (Pinto et al., 2023; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Conversely, anomaly-based or behavioral systems focus on learning a profile of normal network traffic and flagging statistically significant deviations from this established baseline as potential intrusions (Pinto et al., 2023).

The rapid evolution of modern network environments has rendered traditional, static signature-based detection systems increasingly inadequate (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Because modern attackers continuously modify their vectors, obfuscate payloads, and exploit previously undisclosed vulnerabilities, signature databases require constant updates, which is an impractical and unsustainable task in real-world high-throughput environments (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Outages, ransomware attacks, and web-based exploits can cause massive disruptions and tens of millions of dollars in financial damages before a signature can even be generated and distributed (Al Jallad et al., 2020). This structural vulnerability has motivated a paradigm shift toward behavioral anomaly-based models that do not rely on fixed pattern-matching, thereby allowing networks to generalize and adapt to unseen and zero-day threats (Pinto et al., 2023; Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

To achieve this highly desired behavioral adaptability, researchers and practitioners have increasingly turned to Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) techniques to automate and optimize intrusion detection (Ali et al., 2024; Pinto Neto et al., 2025). These learning-based models leverage mathematical algorithms to extract abstract feature representations from massive network data repositories, mapping relationships between input traffic flows and security labels without manual rule engineering (Ali et al., 2024; Pinto Neto et al., 2025). Depending on the training methodology, machine learning models can operate in a supervised manner using labeled historical datasets, or in an unsupervised manner by clustering traffic to identify outliers without any prior classification labels (Ali et al., 2024; Pinto et al., 2023). By dynamically scoring and classifying live traffic flows, these intelligent algorithms provide a robust wall of defense that continuously adapts to changing malicious behaviors (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023).

A diverse array of algorithmic families has been explored in the literature to construct these behavioral detection models, with the most prominent including classical supervised classifiers, unsupervised ensembles, and sequential deep learning architectures. Supervised ensemble tree methods, such as the Random Forest classifier, are highly favored because they deliver exceptional accuracy, strong tabular performance, and interpretable feature importance rankings. On the other hand, the Isolation Forest algorithm represents a powerful unsupervised approach that isolates anomalies through recursive random partitioning, making it uniquely suited for environments where labeled attack data is scarce or where zero-day detection is the primary goal (Chua et al., 2024). Furthermore, sequential deep learning architectures, such as the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, are deployed to capture temporal behavioral patterns over a sliding window of consecutive network flows, which is essential for identifying slow, multi-stage attacks that remain invisible when analyzing individual flows in isolation.

The development and evaluation of these machine learning models are heavily reliant on the quality and realism of benchmark cybersecurity datasets (Maseer et al., 2021). Early academic research and baseline evaluations frequently utilized historical datasets, such as the KDD Cup 1999 and its subsequent resampled version NSL-KDD, but these repositories are now widely recognized as outdated, old-fashioned, and containing redundant records that introduce severe frequency bias (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021). To address these structural deficiencies, the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity developed the widely adopted CICIDS2017 dataset, which simulates realistic user profiles and integrates up-to-date attack scenarios (Maseer et al., 2021). However, because the original CICIDS2017 dataset suffers from extreme class imbalance where benign traffic dominates the files, newer refinements like the IDS2025 balanced dataset have been published to provide a consolidated, cleaned, and properly balanced dataset for robust classifier training.

Despite the excellent accuracy figures reported for machine learning models in academic literature, deploying these systems in practice introduces several persistent and well-documented challenges (Pinto Neto et al., 2025). A primary obstacle in anomaly-based systems is the elevated rate of false-positives, as the system frequently misclassifies legitimate but unusual network traffic as malicious, potentially leading to alert fatigue and a subsequent loss of trust in the system (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021). Additionally, highly complex neural network architectures often suffer from overfitting, meaning they excel during static, offline training over clean data but demonstrate a drastic decrease in classification performance when exposed to real-world networks with concept drift and noise (Pinto Neto et al., 2025). Achieving a high detection accuracy while simultaneously minimizing the false alarm rate remains a central optimization challenge in current cybersecurity research (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Pinto et al., 2023).

Beyond detection metrics, a critical and often neglected aspect of learning-based intrusion detection is the "real-time deployability gap", which represents the discrepancy between offline, static evaluations and the operational constraints of live systems. While many published studies report near-perfect classification metrics, they rarely evaluate the proposed model within a live, time-constrained streaming processing pipeline, ignoring crucial system-level metrics such as end-to-end latency, throughput, and resource consumption. Furthermore, because many advanced machine learning models are designed for high-performance computing environments, they are highly impractical for low-cost, resource-constrained hardware environments where deployment is often most needed (Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Consequently, proving that an intelligent intrusion detection model can process packet streams locally and generate alerts within a bounded, real-time latency window is an engineering requirement that must be rigorously addressed.

This project addresses this research gap directly by focusing on the design, development, and implementation of a real-time network intrusion detection system utilizing behavioral machine learning models. In this study, a network infrastructure serves as the motivating deployment context to which the simulated traffic patterns, extracted from the refined IDS2025 benchmark dataset, are representative. By comparatively evaluating three distinct algorithmic families, consisting of the unsupervised Isolation Forest, the supervised Random Forest, and the sequential Long Short-Term Memory network, this project seeks to map the accuracy-latency trade-off empirically. Ultimately, this research aims to pair the trained behavioral models with an engineered streaming pipeline to demonstrate that robust threat detection can be achieved locally within a restricted hardware and time budget, thereby bridging the gap between theoretical machine learning and practical, real-time network defense.

## **1.2 Problems Statement** 

Despite the substantial advances in network security technologies, conventional signature-based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) remain fundamentally constrained by their reliance on pre-configured databases of known attack fingerprints (Kumar et al., 2025). While signature-based architectures achieve high precision when matching established threats, they are structurally incapable of identifying novel, zero-day, or polymorphic attacks whose signatures have not yet been documented and compiled (Wijethilaka et al., 2025). In modern high-throughput environments where cyber threats evolve rapidly and attackers routinely obfuscate malicious payloads, updating signature rulebooks every few minutes is functionally unfeasible (Al Jallad et al., 2020). Consequently, relying solely on signature matching leaves enterprise and academic network perimeters highly exposed to undisclosed vulnerabilities and zero-day exploits (Kumar et al., 2025).

To overcome these structural limitations, contemporary research has shifted toward behavioral, anomaly-based detection mechanisms driven by machine learning algorithms (Ali et al., 2024; Sharafaldin et al., 2019). However, learning-based approaches introduce severe operational hurdles of their own, most notably an elevated False-Positive Rate (FPR) (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Nocera et al., 2024; Pinto Neto et al., 2025). Because anomaly-based detectors flag any statistical deviation from an established baseline of normal traffic, legitimate but non-routine network operations are frequently misclassified as malicious intrusions (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Sharafaldin et al., 2019). This frequent misclassification triggers severe alert fatigue among network security administrators, causing critical security warnings to be overlooked amidst thousands of false alarms (Nocera et al., 2024; Pinto Neto et al., 2025). Additionally, severe class imbalance in raw network datasets, where benign traffic vastly outnumbers attack records, causes machine learning classifiers to become biased toward majority classes and fail at detecting rare but dangerous intrusions (Hamidou & Adda, 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

A more profound gap in existing literature is the widespread real-time deployability gap, where machine learning models are evaluated almost exclusively in static, offline batch modes (Pinto Neto et al., 2025). Most published academic studies report isolated model inference execution times on static data already loaded into memory, completely ignoring true end-to-end system latency that incorporates data ingestion, feature scaling, model classification, and alert serialization. Furthermore, many advanced deep learning architectures require expensive, high-performance computing clusters, rendering them financially and computationally unfeasible for resource-constrained institutional environments (Pinto Neto et al., 2025; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Operating a behavioral detector on budget-constrained hardware without introducing unacceptable processing delays remains an unaddressed technical challenge in academic research (Panigrahi & Borah, 2025; Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

To solve these combined challenges, this project focuses on designing, developing, and implementing a real-time behavioral network intrusion detection system paired with a live streaming pipeline. Using a refined and rebalanced benchmark dataset (IDS2025), the proposed system addresses class imbalance and minimizes false positives while comparatively evaluating three distinct model families, specifically Isolation Forest, Random Forest, and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. By deploying these models within a decoupled producer-consumer streaming architecture connected to a Streamlit visualization dashboard, the study empirically measures both classification accuracy and true end-to-end processing latency. Ultimately, this project bridges the gap between theoretical machine learning models and practical, time-constrained network defense.

## **1.4 Scope and Limitations**

This study is scoped around the design, development, and empirical evaluation of a real-time behavioral network intrusion detection system built on machine learning techniques. The research focuses exclusively on behavioral, anomaly-oriented traffic classification rather than static signature matching or host-based log auditing, since the aim is to detect deviation from learned normal behavior rather than match traffic against a fixed rule set. Model development is restricted to three algorithms representing distinct paradigms: the unsupervised Isolation Forest, the supervised Random Forest, and the sequential Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network. This selection is deliberate rather than exhaustive, it spans an unsupervised anomaly-based approach, a supervised ensemble approach, and a sequential deep learning approach, so that the comparative evaluation in Chapter Four reflects genuinely different algorithmic strategies rather than minor variations of the same technique. Other model families, including transformer-based architectures and graph neural networks, are acknowledged as viable alternatives but fall outside this study's scope.

The system uses tabular network flow features drawn from the refined IDS2025 benchmark dataset (Panigrahi & Borah, 2025), which provides rebalanced flow records covering benign communication alongside attack vectors including Denial of Service, Distributed Denial of Service, port scanning, brute-force attempts, botnets, and web exploits. Detection is performed at the level of network flow records, aggregated statistical summaries of a connection, rather than on raw packet payloads, meaning the system does not perform deep packet inspection or decrypt and analyze encrypted payload content. The engineering scope covers a complete end-to-end streaming pipeline: a producer module that streams dataset records over a local TCP socket at a configurable interval, a real-time inference engine that applies a fitted preprocessing pipeline before classification, and a live web-based visualization dashboard built with Streamlit (Khorasani et al., 2022) to monitor traffic volume, protocol distribution, confidence scores, and alerts as they occur.

Performance is evaluated across two dimensions. Offline classification performance is measured using precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC on a held-out test partition. System-level performance is measured as end-to-end latency, defined as the interval from a flow's emission by the producer to its prediction reaching the dashboard, and throughput, defined as the number of flows processed per second, both measured under steady-state and burst emission conditions. A target end-to-end latency threshold is defined in Chapter Three and used as the benchmark against which each model's real-time suitability is judged, rather than treating "real-time" as a qualitative label.

To remain feasible within the time and resource constraints of an undergraduate project, several limitations are deliberately accepted rather than overlooked. Live network traffic capture was not performed. This decision reflects three practical constraints rather than one: access to production network infrastructure for capture purposes was not available within the project timeline, capturing and storing real traffic raises data privacy considerations that were judged to be out of proportion to the scope of an undergraduate study, and the packet-capture hardware such a deployment would require was not available. Real-time execution is instead demonstrated through a controlled, socket-based replay of the benchmark dataset that mirrors the timing behavior of live ingestion without requiring any of the above.

Consequently, the detection accuracy and behavioral profiling of the trained models are bounded by the feature distributions and attack categories present in IDS2025. While the models are expected to generalize reasonably to unseen instances of the same attack categories, their performance against attack types not represented in the benchmark corpus, including genuinely novel zero-day techniques, has not been tested and should not be assumed. System implementation and latency benchmarking are conducted on a single local development host rather than a distributed or edge-computing environment; the specific hardware specification used is documented in Chapter Four, and all reported throughput and latency figures should be read as characteristic of that specific machine rather than as universal performance guarantees. Finally, the developed artefact is strictly a detection and visualization system. Active mitigation functions, such as automated firewall rule updates, automated packet dropping, or inline intrusion prevention enforcement, are outside the scope of this research; the system generates alerts for a human operator to act on, it does not act autonomously on the network.

## **LITERATURE REVIEW**

## **2.1 Introduction**

As modern organizational infrastructure becomes increasingly reliant on digital connectivity and distributed networks, establishing a resilient defensive perimeter against sophisticated cyber threats has become a paramount priority (Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Historically, network security relied heavily on signature-based detection mechanisms that match incoming traffic against databases of known attack patterns. Although signature matching achieves minimal false alarms for catalogued exploits, it is structurally incapable of identifying novel, zero-day attacks (Maseer et al., 2021).

This critical limitation has driven a paradigm shift toward behavioral anomaly-based intrusion detection, where machine learning algorithms learn statistical profiles of normal communications and flag significant deviations. Consequently, this chapter reviews the relevant literature to establish a foundation for the proposed project. Section 2.2 explores the conceptual evolution of intrusion detection. Section 2.3 examines the theoretical frameworks of tree-based and sequential machine learning algorithms. Section 2.4 provides an empirical review of cybersecurity datasets and related works, while Section 2.5 synthesizes the literature to identify the real-time operational gap this study addresses.

## **2.2 Conceptual Review**

The conceptual framework underlying machine learning-based intrusion detection encompasses a spectrum of algorithmic paradigms designed to transition network security from static rule matching to adaptive behavioral modeling (Ali, 2024; Pinto Neto et al., 2025). Within this scope, supervised learning techniques utilize labeled network flow traffic to construct predictive decision boundaries capable of discriminating between benign communications and known attack patterns (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2025). Among tree-based ensemble methods, Random Forest is widely evaluated for its capacity to model non-linear tabular feature interactions, reduce prediction variance, and generate interpretable feature importance metrics without incurring excessive computational overhead (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Conversely, unsupervised anomaly detection algorithms address the challenge of unlabeled traffic and novel zero-day threats by identifying instances that deviate significantly from baseline operational profiles (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Specifically, Isolation Forest establishes an efficient unsupervised isolation mechanism by recursively partitioning feature space, making it exceptionally well suited for high-speed streaming environments where anomaly scoring must occur with minimal latency (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024).

To address the temporal dynamics inherent in continuous network streams, the conceptual scope extends to sequential deep learning architectures and multi-model hybrid frameworks (Magar et al., 2025; Volpe et al., 2024). Recurrent neural networks, particularly Long Short-Term Memory networks, incorporate gated memory structures to capture time-series correlations across consecutive packets, enabling the identification of complex, multi-stage attacks that remain invisible when analyzing individual flows in isolation (Suresh Kumar et al., 2025; Volpe et al., 2024). Furthermore, hybrid systems synthesize spatial feature learning with temporal sequence analysis, combining algorithms like Random Forest or Convolutional Neural Networks with recurrent units to improve classification accuracy and minimize false-negative rates (Magar et al., 2025; Pinto Neto et al., 2025). However, operationalizing these conceptual models in live production environments requires balancing predictive precision against strict system-level constraints, including feature engineering overhead, data imbalance mitigation through oversampling, real-time throughput demands, and sub-second pipeline latency (Ali, 2024; Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Yahyaoui et al., 2021).

### **2.2.1 Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS)**

An Intrusion Detection System (IDS) is an automated hardware or software security mechanism that continuously monitors network traffic flows, system activities, and operating system event logs to identify unauthorized access attempts, policy violations, or malicious behaviors (Asharf et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021). Positioned as an essential secondary layer of defense behind perimeter firewalls and access control systems, an IDS reframes security management as an automated surveillance and auditing process designed to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of digital infrastructure (E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). Rather than attempting to block incoming connections at the perimeter passively, an intrusion detection framework actively observes operational behavior across network nodes to detect intrusions that bypass primary access restrictions (Asharf et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024).

The primary purpose of deploying an Intrusion Detection System is to maintain real-time operational visibility across digital assets and ensure the prompt identification of malicious events (Suresh Kumar et al., 2026; Volpe et al., 2024). Specifically, an IDS continuously analyzes inbound and outbound traffic streams to detect cyber threats such as Denial of Service (DoS) attacks, brute-force login attempts, port scanning, and unauthorized privilege escalation (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Upon identifying suspicious or anomalous patterns, the system generates immediate alert notifications for security administrators to facilitate rapid threat containment and incident response (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). Furthermore, an IDS serves a critical forensic purpose by capturing detailed transaction logs, packet headers, and event metadata for retrospective security auditing, while supporting active defense frameworks by signaling decision engines or firewalls to isolate compromised nodes from the broader network (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Volpe et al., 2024).

Intrusion Detection Systems are categorized into distinct operational types based on their underlying threat detection methodologies (Maseer et al., 2021; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). When classified by detection methodology, four primary types exist: signature-based, behavioral anomaly-based, specification-based, and hybrid systems (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Signature-based detection operates by matching real-time packet payloads against a pre-configured database of documented attack fingerprints, delivering high accuracy and minimal false alarms for known threats but failing completely against novel or zero-day exploits (Asharf et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Behavioral anomaly-based detection models a baseline profile of normal network communications using statistical analysis or machine learning, flagging significant statistical deviations as potential security breaches to enable zero-day threat identification (E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Specification-based detection relies on manually configured protocol constraints and operational rules to detect policy violations, while hybrid detection integrates signature matching with behavioral anomaly modeling to optimize overall classification accuracy while suppressing false alarms (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026).

When categorized according to deployment scope, intrusion detection systems are divided into Network-based Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) and Host-based Intrusion Detection Systems (HIDS) (Asharf et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021). A NIDS is strategically deployed at network perimeters or internal chokepoints, where it passively captures and inspects protocol packets transmitted across multiple connected devices without requiring local host software agents (Asharf et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021). In contrast, a HIDS is installed directly on an individual host or endpoint, where it monitors local operating system calls, application event logs, and system file integrity (Asharf et al., 2020; E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024). While NIDS provides macro-level visibility over entire network segments to detect transit threats such as Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) floods, HIDS offers micro-level insight into host execution states, enabling the detection of localized buffer overflows, unauthorized file modifications, and internal privilege escalation (Asharf et al., 2020; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). Additionally, NIDS deployment is non-intrusive and avoids consuming endpoint compute resources, whereas HIDS can inspect unencrypted host data prior to network transmission at the cost of utilizing local CPU and memory capacity (Asharf et al., 2020). Together, NIDS and HIDS establish a complementary, multi-layered defensive posture covering both macro network flows and micro host execution environments (Asharf et al., 2020; E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024).

### **2.2.2 Network Intrusion Detection**

Network Intrusion Detection relies fundamentally on continuous network traffic monitoring, which captures and inspects data packets traversing network segments to evaluate communication health and operational security (Ali et al., 2024; E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Rather than inspecting raw packet payloads individually, which introduces immense processing and memory overhead, modern detection architectures aggregate related packets sharing common transport attributes, such as source IP, destination IP, source port, destination port, and protocol, into structured units known as network flows (Volpe et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). Network flow analysis extracts statistical and temporal summary features, including flow duration, packet arrival rates, byte counts, and TCP flag configurations, providing a compact, high-dimensional representation of communication patterns that allows machine learning algorithms to evaluate traffic behavior efficiently across high-speed streaming environments (E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021).

Within monitored traffic streams, malicious activities represent deliberate unauthorized actions or anomalous protocol behaviors designed to compromise the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of network resources (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026; Volpe et al., 2024). These threats encompass a broad taxonomy of attack vectors, ranging from disruptive Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) floods and resource exhaustion attempts to exploratory probing attacks like port scanning and network reconnaissance (E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Additionally, sophisticated threats involve web application exploits such as SQL injection and Cross-Site Scripting (XSS), credential brute-forcing, unauthorized remote-to-local (R2L) access, and malicious botnet communications (E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Behavioral anomaly detectors evaluate network flows against learned baseline representations of legitimate communication to identify subtle statistical deviations indicative of both established attacks and novel zero-day exploits (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026).

A fundamental distinction exists between the operational objectives of detecting intrusions and preventing them within network defense architectures (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Volpe et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) operate primarily as passive, detective controls that inspect streamed network flows to identify suspicious patterns, generate security alerts, and collect forensic log evidence for security administrators without interrupting active data transmission (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026; Volpe et al., 2024). In contrast, Intrusion Prevention Systems (IPS) act as active, inline defensive enforcement mechanisms positioned directly in the communication path, equipped with the authority to automatically drop malicious packets, reset unauthorized connections, or reconfigure firewall rules in real time upon threat identification (Volpe et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). While intrusion detection provides essential situational awareness and low-risk behavioral analysis without introducing inline network failure points, intrusion prevention extends this capability by enforcing automated active mitigation to stop cyber threats before they penetrate protected internal endpoints (Volpe et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

### **2.2.3 Evolution from Rule-Based to Learning-Based Intrusion Detection**

The paradigm of network security has undergone a significant transformation, evolving from rigid rule-based systems to adaptive machine learning-based detection frameworks (Ali et al., 2024; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Early intrusion detection architectures relied heavily on expert-crafted rules, static heuristics, and signature databases to flag known malicious activity (Ali et al., 2024; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). While signature-matching platforms like Snort and Zeek deliver high precision and low false-positive rates for catalogued exploits, they require continuous manual updates and remain structurally incapable of detecting novel, zero-day, or polymorphic attacks (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). As modern network infrastructures expanded across cloud, edge, and mobile environments, the sheer volume, velocity, and complexity of network traffic rendered static rulebooks impractical, driving a transition toward learning-based models (Ali et al., 2024; Pinto Neto et al., 2025).

Learning-based Intrusion Detection Systems reframe security monitoring as an automated statistical pattern recognition problem, leveraging artificial intelligence algorithms to extract complex feature representations directly from high-dimensional network flow data (Ali et al., 2024; Maseer et al., 2021). A central driver of this transition is behavioral anomaly detection, which establishes a baseline profile of legitimate network activity and flags statistical deviations as potential security incidents, thereby enabling the autonomous identification of unrecorded threat vectors (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026).

Within learning-based intrusion detection, supervised learning represents the most widely deployed methodology for classifying network traffic into benign or specific attack categories (Ali et al., 2024; Maseer et al., 2021). Supervised algorithms are trained on historical, labeled datasets containing pre-classified traffic records, allowing models to construct explicit decision boundaries that map input flow vectors to security labels (Ali et al., 2024; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Prominent supervised algorithms in intrusion detection literature include Random Forest, Support Vector Machines (SVM), Decision Trees, Naïve Bayes, and Deep Neural Networks (DNN) (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). In empirical benchmark evaluations, supervised models achieve exceptional overall classification accuracy, high precision, and low error rates when detecting well-represented threat types such as Denial of Service (DoS) and port scanning (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Maseer et al., 2021). However, the effectiveness of supervised learning is fundamentally constrained by its dependence on extensive human-annotated data, as manually labeling massive streams of modern network traffic is time-consuming, expensive, and error-prone (Pinto Neto et al., 2025; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). Furthermore, supervised classifiers tend to struggle when confronted with severe class imbalance or zero-day threats that lack corresponding historical training labels (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

To overcome the limitations of labeled training requirements and enhance zero-day threat detection, unsupervised learning algorithms have been increasingly integrated into anomaly-based intrusion detection frameworks (Ali et al., 2024; Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024). Unsupervised learning operates directly on unlabeled network flows, utilizing mathematical properties such as spatial distance, density distribution, or recursive feature isolation to uncover latent patterns and cluster traffic without prior classification labels (Ali et al., 2024; Chua et al., 2024). Standard unsupervised approaches include K-Means clustering, Expectation-Maximization (EM), One-Class Support Vector Machines, and Isolation Forest (Chua et al., 2024; Maseer et al., 2021; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). Because unsupervised models evaluate whether incoming traffic conforms to normal statistical clusters or resides in sparse regions of the feature space, they possess an inherent capability to detect novel, uncatalogued zero-day exploits and insider anomalies (Chua et al., 2024; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). Nevertheless, unsupervised methods generally exhibit lower multi-class classification precision compared to supervised counterparts and frequently suffer from higher False-Positive Rates (FPR) when benign network behavior shifts dynamically (Ali et al., 2024; Al Jallad et al., 2020; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023).

The adoption of machine learning in network intrusion detection offers major operational advantages over traditional rule-based systems, including automated feature extraction, high predictive accuracy, adaptability to changing network conditions, and the capability to generalize across high-dimensional data streams (Ali et al., 2024; Pinto Neto et al., 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). By eliminating the need for manual rule authoring, learning-based architectures allow security infrastructure to scale alongside modern cloud, Internet of Things (IoT), and high-speed network environments (Pinto Neto et al., 2025; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Despite these significant benefits, machine learning-based intrusion detection faces persistent challenges and practical limitations in real-world deployments (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Pinto Neto et al., 2025).

The primary hurdle remains elevated false-positive rates, which can trigger severe alert fatigue among security operations personnel (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021). Additionally, severe dataset class imbalance causes models to overfit toward majority benign traffic while missing rare but critical attack classes like User-to-Root (U2R) or Remote-to-Local (R2L) exploits (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Maseer et al., 2021). Furthermore, the high computational complexity of complex deep learning algorithms introduces processing latency and resource overhead that can hinder real-time deployment on budget-constrained edge devices, while the opaque nature of non-linear models obscures decision logic and reduces administrative trust (Ali et al., 2024; Pinto Neto et al., 2025; Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

### **2.2.4 Signature-Based versus Behavioral Anomaly-Based Detection**

Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) are fundamentally categorized into two main paradigms based on their threat detection methodologies, specifically signature-based detection and behavioral anomaly-based detection (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Signature-based detection, also known as misuse detection, operates by comparing real-time network packet headers and payload contents against a pre-configured database of catalogued attack fingerprints or rules (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). When incoming traffic matches a known signature, an alert is triggered with exceptionally high classification accuracy and minimal false alarms, making signature matching highly effective for established threats (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). However, signature-based detection suffers from a critical structural limitation because it is completely blind to novel, zero-day, or obfuscated attacks for which no predefined rule exists in the database (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Conversely, behavioral anomaly-based detection shifts the focus from static pattern matching to baseline modeling by establishing a profile of normal network communications and flagging statistically significant deviations as potential security breaches (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). This behavioral approach enables anomaly-based systems to generalize across unseen traffic distributions and detect zero-day exploits without relying on prior threat signatures (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026).

To operationalize behavioral anomaly detection, the system must first model normal traffic behavior across the monitored network infrastructure (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026).

Normal traffic behavior encompasses the consistent, predictable interaction patterns observed during legitimate user activity and routine system communications, represented through statistical distributions of flow duration, packet sizes, protocol flag configurations, and inter-arrival times (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Once this baseline profile is established using machine learning or statistical techniques, the anomaly detector continuously compares live streaming flows against the learned baseline (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). When incoming network traffic exhibits atypical attribute values, such as abnormal data transfer volumes, unusual port access sequences, or unexpected request frequencies, the system identifies these variations as deviations from learned normal behavior (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). These statistical deviations signal potential security incidents, malicious reconnaissance, or unauthorized access attempts that warrant further inspection (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026).

In machine learning-based anomaly detection frameworks, deviations from learned patterns are quantified using numerical values known as anomaly scores (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). Algorithms compute these scores based on spatial distance, probability distribution, or recursive feature isolation (Chua et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). For example, in unsupervised models like Isolation Forest, anomalous traffic instances reside in sparse regions of the feature space and require fewer recursive splits to isolate, resulting in shorter path lengths that map directly to higher anomaly scores (Chua et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). To make classification decisions, the detection engine compares the computed anomaly score against a predefined decision threshold (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Traffic flows with anomaly scores exceeding the established threshold are classified as suspicious or malicious and trigger immediate security alerts, whereas traffic instances falling below the threshold are passed as normal communications (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026).

Despite the distinct advantage of uncovering unrecorded zero-day exploits, behavioral anomaly-based intrusion detection faces a major operational challenge in managing false-positive rates (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). A false positive occurs when legitimate, benign network traffic is misclassified as a malicious intrusion because its features deviate from the learned baseline profile (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). This misclassification frequently stems from non-routine benign events, such as temporary network congestion, software updates, or benign seasonal traffic spikes that were absent from the training dataset (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). High false-positive rates create severe alert fatigue for security operations personnel, who become overwhelmed by thousands of false alarms and may inadvertently overlook critical warnings or raise alert thresholds, thereby leaving the network exposed (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024). Consequently, optimizing anomaly detection models to achieve high zero-day sensitivity while suppressing false-positive rates remains a central research requirement in modern network security (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026).

### **2.2.5 Streaming Analytics and Real-Time Deployability Concepts**

Streaming data in network intrusion detection represents a continuous, high-velocity flow of unbounded packets and transaction records transmitted dynamically across network interfaces (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Unlike traditional static data processing, where complete historical datasets are stored in memory prior to analysis, streaming environments generate data with non-stationary distributions, high arrival speeds, and fluctuating traffic volumes (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). To operate effectively within this dynamic environment, intrusion detection systems rely on continuous inference, a process where pre-trained or online machine learning models sequentially ingest live data streams, extract feature representations, and generate instantaneous classification predictions on incoming packet batches or sliding flow windows without interrupting data transmission (E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Volpe et al., 2024; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Continuous inference transitions security monitoring from static, offline retrospective analysis to active, real-time threat evaluation, allowing automated detectors to evaluate network health continuously as communication flows pass through transport sockets (Volpe et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021).

The operational viability of real-time streaming analytics is dictated primarily by system-level performance parameters, specifically processing latency and flow throughput (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Processing latency represents the total end-to-end time delay consumed by the system during packet ingestion, feature normalization, model classification, and prediction output (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). System throughput measures the volume of network flow records or data packets processed successfully per second under steady-state and burst traffic conditions (E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). While conventional machine learning research evaluates algorithms almost exclusively on offline classification accuracy, real-time deployability demands a strict balance between predictive accuracy and computational latency (E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). If the feature extraction or inference pipeline introduces sub-second processing delays exceeding the packet arrival rate, queuing bottlenecks develop, leading to buffer overflow, packet drops, and degraded security monitoring (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021).

When continuous inference engines detect anomalous behavioral patterns or malicious traffic flows that exceed designated classification thresholds, the streaming architecture triggers automated alert generation protocols (Volpe et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Alert generation encompasses the immediate serialization and dispatch of threat notifications containing critical contextual metadata, such as source IP addresses, destination ports, flow durations, attack classifications, and model confidence scores (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). In modern real-time deployments, generated alerts are dispatched simultaneously across multiple channels, including live web-based visualization dashboards (such as Streamlit or Grafana) for administrative visibility, automated email alerts for remote incident response, and physical signaling devices like LEDs or buzzers on resource-constrained hardware (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Furthermore, in hybrid intrusion detection and prevention architectures, alert signals act as virtual sensing triggers that pass execution commands to inline decision engines, enabling the automated dropping or blocking of malicious packet buffers in real time (Volpe et al., 2024).

A critical methodological distinction exists between replaying pre-recorded dataset traffic and monitoring live operational network traffic in real-time deployment evaluations (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024). Replaying dataset traffic involves reading historical, benchmark flow records from disk or database repositories and streaming them sequentially across a local socket connection to simulate live network data ingestion (Volpe et al., 2024; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). This replay approach provides a controlled, highly reproducible testing environment that enables researchers to measure pipeline throughput and system latency safely without risking production network disruptions, violating administrative access policies, or exposing user privacy (Volpe et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). In contrast, monitoring live network traffic requires deploying sniffer agents directly onto physical or virtual network interface cards to capture uncurated, high-velocity packet streams in real time (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). While live monitoring exposes the detection model to real-world operational challenges, such as unmanaged network noise, hardware resource bounds, and dynamic concept drift, controlled streaming replay serves as a necessary, standardized engineering proxy for validating end-to-end real-time pipeline performance before production deployment (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024).

## **2.3 Theoretical Framework**

The theoretical foundation of machine learning-based network intrusion detection relies on distinct algorithmic paradigms designed to process tabular attributes, isolate behavioral anomalies, and capture temporal dependencies across streaming traffic (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Magar et al., 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Supervised ensemble learning, exemplified by Random Forest, is grounded in decision tree theory and bootstrap aggregation (bagging), where multiple decision trees are constructed using randomized feature subsets to map non-linear relationships across tabular flow attributes (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Pandey et al., 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). By aggregating predictions across constituent trees via majority voting, Random Forest minimizes variance, reduces overfitting, and establishes robust decision boundaries for classifying structured network features such as packet lengths, flow durations, and flag counts (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). In contrast, unsupervised anomaly isolation via Isolation Forest shifts away from distance-based or density-based metrics, operating instead on the principle of recursive random feature partitioning (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Lua et al., 2024). Because anomalous network traffic flows reside in sparse regions of the feature space and differ significantly from regular communication baselines, they require fewer partitioning splits to isolate (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Lua et al., 2024). Consequently, anomalous instances yield shorter average path lengths within isolation trees, which directly translate into quantitative anomaly scores capable of flagging novel zero-day exploits without relying on prior attack labels (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Lua et al., 2024).

Sequential deep learning via Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks addresses a fundamental limitation of static classifiers by modeling time-series dependencies across consecutive network flows (Magar et al., 2025; Volpe et al., 2024). As an advanced extension of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), LSTM architectures incorporate specialized memory cells governed by input, forget, and output gates that selectively retain or discard historical context over extended temporal horizons (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024). This gating mechanism resolves vanishing gradient limitations during training and enables the model to identify complex, multi-stage attacks such as distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) floods or low-and-slow reconnaissance that appear benign when individual packets are evaluated in isolation (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024).

Combining spatial feature classification (Random Forest), zero-day anomaly isolation (Isolation Forest), and temporal sequence modeling (LSTM) establishes a comprehensive theoretical basis for real-time intrusion detection (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Magar et al., 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Together, these three mathematical frameworks allow security architectures to systematically balance high detection precision against real-time streaming constraints and pipeline computational latency (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Magar et al., 2025; Volpe et al., 2024).

### **2.3.1 Theory of Unsupervised Anomaly Isolation (Isolation Forest)**

Traditional anomaly detection methods build a profile of normal behavior using distance-based techniques such as k-Nearest Neighbors or density estimation such as Local Outlier Factor (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Lua et al., 2024). These methods have quadratic time complexity, O(n²), and scale poorly to high-dimensional, high-velocity streaming network data (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Zhang et al., 2023). The Isolation Forest (iForest) algorithm, introduced by Liu et al. (2008, 2012), takes a different approach. Instead of measuring distances or modeling the density of normal data, it isolates anomalies directly through recursive random partitioning. It rests on two premises about malicious traffic: anomalies are few, and their attribute values differ markedly from the normal baseline (Liu et al., 2008).

An Isolation Forest is an ensemble of binary isolation trees (iTrees), each built from a random subsample of the data, typically ψ = 256 records. Subsampling reduces memory use and limits swamping (normal traffic wrongly flagged as anomalous) and masking (malicious traffic hidden inside normal clusters) (Liu et al., 2008; Lua et al., 2024). To build a tree, a feature q is chosen at random, then a split value p is drawn uniformly at random between the minimum and maximum of q in the current sample. Records with values at or below p go to the left child node and the rest go to the right, and the process repeats on each child. Splitting stops when a node holds a single record, when all records in a node are identical, or when the tree reaches the maximum height L = ⌈log₂ ψ⌉ (Liu et al., 2008). Anomalous flows sit in sparse regions of the feature space, so they are separated after only a few splits and end up near the root. Benign flows form dense clusters that need many splits to separate, so they end up deep in the tree.

This idea is quantified through the path length h(x), the number of edges from the root of a tree to the leaf where record x ends up. Averaging h(x) over all t trees gives the expected path length E(h(x)). To compare path lengths across different sample sizes n, E(h(x)) is normalized by c(n), the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree (Liu et al., 2008):

$c(n) = 2H(n - 1) - \left( \frac{2(n - 1)}{n} \right)$

Here H(k) is the harmonic number, approximated as ln(k) + γ, where γ ≈ 0.5772 is Euler’s constant. The anomaly score of record x is then:

$S(x,n) = 2^{- \frac{E(h(x))}{c(n)}}$

where:

- S(x, n) is the anomaly score of observation x.

- h(x) is the path length required to isolate observation x.

- E(h(x)) is the average path length across the isolation trees.

- n is the sample size used to construct the trees.

- c(n) is the expected path length used for normalization.

The score lies between 0 and 1. A score close to 1 means E(h(x)) is very small, so the record is isolated almost immediately and is very likely an anomaly. A score close to 0.5 means E(h(x)) is close to c(n), so the record shows no distinct anomalous character. A score close to 0 means the path is long and the record lies deep inside a dense cluster of normal traffic (Liu et al., 2008).

These properties suit Isolation Forest to network intrusion detection, particularly for zero-day threats and streaming traffic. Because it relies on random splits rather than pairwise distance calculations, training costs O(tψ log ψ) and scoring a single flow costs O(t log ψ), which does not grow with the size of the dataset. This is what makes real-time scoring over a socket feasible (Liu et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2023). The algorithm works directly on tabular flow attributes such as flow duration, packet arrival rates, and byte counts, and needs no attack labels during training. Flows whose score exceeds a chosen threshold λ (S(x, n) ≥ λ) are flagged as potential intrusions and passed to the dashboard as alerts, covering unauthorized probing, DDoS bursts, and previously unseen exploits (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Lua et al., 2024).

### **2.3.2 Theory of Tree-Based Ensemble Classifiers (Random Forest)**

Random Forest is a prominent supervised ensemble learning algorithm constructed from an aggregation of multiple decision trees to perform robust classification over high-dimensional data (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; Pandey et al., 2025). Individual decision tree learning operates by recursively partitioning feature space based on node impurity metrics, such as Gini impurity or information gain entropy, to create decision rules that separate data instances into homogeneous classes (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Mahbooba et al., 2021; Maseer et al., 2021). However, standalone decision trees are highly sensitive to small variations in training data, making them prone to high variance and overfitting when evaluating complex network traffic distributions (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Mahbooba et al., 2021; Maseer et al., 2021). Random Forest resolves tree instability through bootstrap aggregation (bagging) combined with random feature subspace selection (Breiman, 2001; Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Pandey et al., 2025). In this ensemble framework, each constituent decision tree $T_{i}$ is trained on an independently drawn bootstrap sample created by sampling the training dataset with replacement (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Pandey et al., 2025). Furthermore, at each split node within a tree, the algorithm considers only a randomly selected subset of $m$ features from the total $M$ attributes (where $m \ll M$, typically $m =$), preventing dominant features from dictating every split, decorrelating individual trees, and improving overall model generalization across unseen data streams (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Pandey et al., 2025).

During the classification phase, an input test sample $x$ represented as a high-dimensional vector of extracted network flow attributes is passed independently down every decision tree within the forest (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; Pandey et al., 2025). Each tree evaluates the feature vector through its split conditions until reaching a terminal leaf node, casting a discrete class vote $T_{i}(x)$ for the sample (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Pandey et al., 2025). The ensemble aggregates these individual predictions across all constituent trees via majority voting to determine the final ensemble output (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Maseer et al., 2021). Mathematically, for an ensemble comprising $N$ decision trees, the predicted class label $\widehat{y}$ for input vector $x$ is formulated as:

where $C$ denotes the set of categorical target labels (e.g., Benign, DoS, PortScan, Brute Force), $T_{i}(x)$ represents the class assigned by the $i$-th decision tree, and $I( \cdot )$ is an indicator function that equals $1$ when $T_{i}(x) = c$ and $0$ otherwise (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; Pandey et al., 2025). In the context of this project, Random Forest leverages labeled network-flow records encompassing statistical parameters such as flow duration, packet inter-arrival times, source-to-destination byte ratios, time-to-live values, and TCP flag rates to learn predictive decision rules that map tabular flow features directly to threat categories represented in the training dataset, enabling precise real-time classification of streaming traffic as benign or malicious (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Pandey et al., 2025).

An important distinction must be emphasized between Random Forest and Isolation Forest regarding their fundamental learning mechanisms and training requirements (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Lua et al., 2024; Maseer et al., 2021). Random Forest is ordinarily a supervised classifier that requires a fully annotated dataset containing explicit target labels for both benign traffic and specific attack classes during its offline training phase (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Maseer et al., 2021). It constructs discriminative decision boundaries that map flow features to these pre-labeled categories, making its predictive performance dependent on the presence and balance of labeled attack instances in the training corpus (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Pandey et al., 2025). In contrast, Isolation Forest is an unsupervised anomaly-detection algorithm that operates without target labels or prior class definitions, relying instead on recursive random feature partitioning to measure instance path lengths and isolate structural outliers from regular communication baselines (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Lua et al., 2024). Acknowledging this distinction clarifies that while Random Forest excels at high-accuracy multi-class categorization over known threat distributions, Isolation Forest provides complementary zero-day detection by isolating rare structural anomalies without relying on pre-existing supervised labels (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Lua et al., 2024; Magar et al., 2025).

### **2.3.3 Theory of Sequential Deep Learning: Long Short-Term Memory Networks**

While conventional machine learning classifiers evaluate network traffic records as isolated, independent observations, modern cyber threats often unfold dynamically across sequences of consecutive communications (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026; Volpe et al., 2024). Standalone tree-based models, such as Random Forest and Decision Trees, process tabular feature vectors independently, ignoring temporal relationships between adjacent traffic flows (Magar et al., 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) address this structural limitation by introducing cyclic feedback connections that create an internal memory state $h_{t}$ (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026; Volpe et al., 2024). Unlike feedforward architectures, an RNN processes sequential inputs iteratively, passing the hidden state output from time step $t - 1$ into the current computation at time step $t$ alongside the new input vector $x_{t}$ (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). This recurrent loop allows the network to maintain contextual memory across ordered observations, making RNNs suitable for analyzing time-series data and sequential communications (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026; Volpe et al., 2024).

Despite their theoretical suitability for sequence processing, standard vanilla RNNs struggle when trained on long temporal sequences due to the vanishing and exploding gradient problems (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026; Volpe et al., 2024). During Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT), gradients propagated backward across numerous recurrent time steps undergo repeated matrix multiplications (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024). When weight values are less than unity, gradients decay exponentially toward zero, preventing the network from updating weights for earlier time steps and causing the model to forget long-term context (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024). To overcome this fundamental degradation in long-term dependencies, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997) designed the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network architecture (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024). LSTM networks mitigate vanishing gradients by replacing simple recurrent units with specialized memory cells that maintain a constant error flow along a dedicated cell state pathway (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024).

The core architecture of an LSTM cell relies on an explicit memory state $C_{t}$ regulated by three multiplicative gating mechanisms, the forget gate $f_{t}$, the input gate $i_{t}$, and the output gate $o_{t}$, where the cell state $C_{t}$ acts as a memory highway carrying historical information across long temporal horizons with minimal linear attenuation (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024). The process begins with the forget gate $f_{t}$, which evaluates the previous hidden state $h_{t - 1}$ and current input $x_{t}$ through a sigmoid activation function to output a value between $0$ and $1$, determining what fraction of information from the previous cell state $C_{t - 1}$ should be discarded or retained (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024). Simultaneously, the input gate $i_{t}$ and candidate cell state ${\widetilde{C}}_{t}$ determine what new information to store in the cell state, using a sigmoid layer to decide which features to update while a hyperbolic tangent ($\tanh$) layer generates a candidate vector ${\widetilde{C}}_{t}$ of new values (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024). These components then update the old cell state $C_{t - 1}$ into the new cell state $C_{t}$ by multiplying $C_{t - 1}$ element-wise with $f_{t}$ and adding the product of $i_{t}$ and ${\widetilde{C}}_{t}$ (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024). Finally, the output gate $o_{t}$ and hidden state $h_{t}$ control what portion of the internal cell state is emitted to the output, applying a sigmoid function to filter the input while $C_{t}$ passes through a $\tanh$ function to map values between $- 1$ and $1$, ultimately yielding the updated hidden state $h_{t}$ through their element-wise product (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024).

The mathematical operations governing the internal state transitions of an LSTM cell at time step $t$ are expressed by the following system of equations (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024)1315:

$f_{t} = \sigma(W_{f} \cdot \lbrack h_{t - 1},x_{t}\rbrack + b_{f})$

$i_{t} = \sigma(W_{i} \cdot \lbrack h_{t - 1},x_{t}\rbrack + b_{i})$

${\widetilde{C}}_{t} = \tanh(W_{C} \cdot \lbrack h_{t - 1},x_{t}\rbrack + b_{C})$

$C_{t} = f_{t} \odot C_{t - 1} + i_{t} \odot {\widetilde{C}}_{t}$

$o_{t} = \sigma(W_{o} \cdot \lbrack h_{t - 1},x_{t}\rbrack + b_{o})$

$h_{t} = o_{t} \odot \tanh(C_{t})$

where:

- $f_{t}$, $i_{t}$, and $o_{t}$ represent the activation vectors of the forget, input, and output gates at time step $t$ (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024)1013.

- $C_{t}$ is the updated cell state vector, and ${\widetilde{C}}_{t}$ is the candidate cell state vector (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024)1013.

- $h_{t}$ denotes the output hidden state vector, and $x_{t}$ is the input feature vector at time step $t$ (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024)1013.

- $W_{f},W_{i},W_{C},W_{o}$ represent the learnable weight matrices, and $b_{f},b_{i},b_{C},b_{o}$ denote the corresponding bias vectors for each gate (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024)1315.

- $\sigma( \cdot )$ represents the logistic sigmoid activation function, $\tanh( \cdot )$ denotes the hyperbolic tangent activation function, and $\odot$ specifies the Hadamard element-wise matrix multiplication operator (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024)1213.

In network intrusion detection, temporal relationships matter significantly because many complex cyberattacks manifest as distributed or multi-stage sequences rather than isolated anomalous events (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026; Volpe et al., 2024). Threat vectors such as Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) floods, low-and-slow port scans, brute-force authentication attacks, and lateral reconnaissance rely on repeated or structured traffic exchanges over time (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024). An individual flow record evaluated in isolation may exhibit feature values identical to benign traffic, but when analyzed as part of an ordered temporal sequence, the underlying malicious pattern becomes evident (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026).

An ordinary tabular dataset (such as CICIDS2017, CSE-CIC-IDS2018, or UNSW-NB15) stores flow summary records as independent rows without an explicit sequence structure (Suresh Kumar et al., 2026; Volpe et al., 2024). Therefore, connecting LSTM deep learning models to a real-time intrusion detection project requires establishing a structured sequence transformation pipeline (Magar et al., 2025; Volpe et al., 2024). First, tabular network flow records must be chronologically ordered according to packet arrival timestamps or flow start times (Volpe et al., 2024). Next, a sliding window technique with a fixed lookback size $W$ (such as $W = 50$ consecutive flows) is applied over the ordered data stream (Volpe et al., 2024). This sliding window restructures $2D$ tabular matrices of shape $(samples,features)$ into $3D$ tensor sequences of shape $(samples,window\_ size,features)$ (Volpe et al., 2024). By passing these constructed flow sequences into the LSTM architecture, the model learns contextual temporal dependencies across time steps $t - W + 1$ to $t$, enabling real-time detection of sequence-based network threats (Magar et al., 2025; Volpe et al., 2024).

## **2.4 Review of Related Works**

The empirical literature on machine learning-based network intrusion detection focuses on validating algorithmic performance across benchmark datasets, comparing learning paradigms, and assessing real-time deployment feasibility (Ali et al., 2024; Maseer et al., 2021; E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Over the past two decades, experimental evaluations have transitioned from legacy repositories, such as KDD Cup 99 and NSL-KDD, to contemporary flow-based benchmarks including UNSW-NB15, CICIDS2017, and CSE-CIC-IDS2018 (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Maseer et al., 2021). While numerous empirical studies report classification accuracies exceeding 95% under static batch testing conditions, a rigorous comparative review reveals significant variations in how researchers handle feature selection, hyperparameter optimization, and severe class imbalance through synthetic oversampling techniques like SMOTE (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Analyzing these experimental frameworks is essential for identifying model generalization limits, false positive trends, and performance trade-offs across supervised, unsupervised, and hybrid architectures (Maseer et al., 2021; E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024).

In addition to offline dataset evaluations, a growing body of empirical research examines the operationalization of machine learning models within live streaming environments and resource-constrained hardware deployments (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Experimental studies in this domain measure critical system-level metrics, such as end-to-end processing latency, packet throughput, CPU and memory utilization, and automated alert response times across stream processing engines like Apache Flink and hardware platforms such as Raspberry Pi (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). By reviewing existing experimental literature across dataset benchmarks, algorithmic implementations, and real-time streaming pipelines, this section synthesizes key empirical findings to highlight established state-of-the-art results while pinpointing persistent operational gaps in current intrusion detection systems (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Maseer et al., 2021; Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

### **2.4.1 Historical Datasets versus Contemporary Flow-Based Benchmarks (CICIDS2017 & IDS2025)**

Early intrusion detection research predominantly utilized legacy datasets, such as KDD Cup 99 and NSL-KDD, to train and evaluate machine learning models (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021; E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024). KDD Cup 99, constructed from raw packet captures generated in the 1998 DARPA testbed, contained 41 connection attributes categorized into four primary attack groups: Denial of Service (DoS), Probing, User-to-Root (U2R), and Remote-to-Local (R2L) (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). However, subsequent analytical evaluations exposed critical flaws in KDD Cup 99, most notably an overwhelming proportion of duplicate records (78% in the training set and 75% in the testing set), which introduced severe frequency bias and caused classification models to overfit to repeated patterns (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021). To address these structural deficiencies, Tavallaee et al. (2009) introduced NSL-KDD by removing duplicate entries and selecting balanced record subsets (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025). Although NSL-KDD improved cross-validation reliability, both KDD Cup 99 and NSL-KDD are now widely regarded as obsolete because their synthetic traffic profiles reflect outdated network architectures and lack modern threat vectors, such as web application exploits, mobile malware, and encrypted transport communications (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021; E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024).

To address the obsolescence of legacy benchmarks, contemporary research shifted toward flow-based datasets, most notably the CICIDS2017 benchmark developed by the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Magar et al., 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; Pandey et al., 2025). Generated within a realistic network environment capturing multi-day user behaviors across protocols like HTTP, HTTPS, FTP, SSH, and email, CICIDS2017 utilizes the CICFlowMeter tool to extract over 80 statistical flow features, including flow duration, inter-arrival times, byte rates, and TCP flag counts (Magar et al., 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; Pandey et al., 2025). This flow-based representation enables models to capture complex multi-stage attack scenarios, such as DoS/DDoS, brute-force login attempts, botnet activity, and web application attacks (Magar et al., 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; Pandey et al., 2025). However, extensive benchmark analyses revealed that raw CICIDS2017 data suffers from extreme class imbalance, where benign traffic heavily dominates attack categories, as well as missing or infinite numerical values, file fragmentation, and label inconsistencies across daily capture files (Magar et al., 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; Pandey et al., 2025; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023).

To resolve these structural deficiencies, Panigrahi and Borah (2025) introduced the IDS2025 dataset as a refined, standardized benchmark for evaluating modern network intrusion detection systems (Panigrahi & Borah, 2025). The creation of IDS2025 addresses the corrupted records, missing values, and labeling artifacts found in earlier datasets by applying rigorous cleaning pipelines, feature verification, and balanced class distributions (Panigrahi & Borah, 2025). By eliminating noise and standardizing attribute definitions across attack categories, IDS2025 provides a reliable foundation for evaluating machine learning algorithms without risking model distortion caused by corrupted input features (Panigrahi & Borah, 2025). This systematic dataset refinement reflects a broader consensus in cybersecurity literature: effective intrusion detection models require clean, verified, and well-documented benchmark data to ensure that reported performance gains stem from genuine threat learning rather than dataset-specific artifacts (Maseer et al., 2021; Panigrahi & Borah, 2025; E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024).

Beyond dataset selection, empirical studies demonstrate that dataset quality, class imbalance, data leakage, and generalizability fundamentally govern the practical effectiveness of intrusion detection models (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024). Class imbalance is an inherent characteristic of network security data, as benign communications vastly outnumber rare malicious events like Infiltration or Heartbleed exploits (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). When models are trained on imbalanced data without adjustment, they tend to favor the majority benign class, yielding misleadingly high accuracy scores while missing critical attack instances (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). To counteract this, researchers apply oversampling techniques such as SMOTE on training partitions to balance class representation and improve sensitivity to minority threats (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Furthermore, preventing data leakage requires strict methodological separation, ensuring that feature scaling, normalization, and oversampling are applied exclusively to training data prior to testing (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Maintaining high data quality and rigorous preprocessing is essential for producing models that generalize reliably to live production networks rather than failing when exposed to uncurated operational environments (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021; E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Panigrahi & Borah, 2025).

### **2.4.2 Review of Machine Learning Models in Static/Offline NIDS**

Empirical research on static and offline Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) has comprehensively evaluated supervised, unsupervised, and deep learning algorithms across diverse benchmark datasets (Ali et al., 2024; Maseer et al., 2021; Pinto Neto et al., 2025). Among tree-based supervised classifiers, Random Forest (RF) is consistently highlighted as a top-performing algorithm for tabular flow classification due to its ability to model complex non-linear feature interactions and minimize prediction variance (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Maseer et al., 2021). In an empirical evaluation on the NSL-KDD dataset, Hamidou and Mehdi (2025) applied Optuna hyperparameter optimization alongside Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), achieving a classification accuracy of 99.80% and a Receiver Operating Characteristic Area Under the Curve (ROC-AUC) of 0.9988, outperforming XGBoost (99.79%) and Deep Neural Networks (98.65%). Similarly, Maseer et al. (2021) evaluated Random Forest on the CICIDS2017 dataset using balanced class weights, reporting 99.54% accuracy, 99.56% precision, and a 99.55% F1-score. In a comparative evaluation conducted on CICIDS2017 flow data, Suresh Kumar et al. (2026) demonstrated that Random Forest achieved 97.20% accuracy, significantly outperforming traditional baseline models such as Logistic Regression (89.20%), Decision Trees (91.80%), and Support Vector Machines (93.50%). These studies confirm that Random Forest provides exceptional classification accuracy on structured tabular features like packet lengths, flow durations, and TCP flag rates.

To address the challenge of unlabeled traffic streams and zero-day threat detection, empirical studies have investigated unsupervised anomaly isolation models, focusing primarily on Isolation Forest (iForest) and its optimized variants (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Lua et al., 2024). Lua et al. (2024) implemented Isolation Forest to detect web traffic anomalies within a dataset of over one million e-commerce weblog records, achieving an accuracy of 93%, precision of 95%, recall of 90%, and an F1-score of 92%, while executing model prediction in just 23.62 seconds. To enhance iForest against high-dimensional noise, Elsaid and Binbusayyis (2024) developed the Optimized iForest-based Intrusion Detection System (OIFIDS), which incorporates Enhanced Harris Hawks Optimization (ERHHO) for feature selection. OIFIDS achieved detection accuracies of 95.6% on NSL-KDD, 94.8% on UNSW-NB15, and 99.0% on CICIDS2018, outperforming standard iForest (91.0%) and Autoencoder combined with iForest (95.4%). Furthermore, Real-Time Malicious Traffic Detection (2024) evaluated Isolation Forest under non-stationary network conditions, observing up to a 10% performance gap between static training datasets and varying test environments, which led to the development of an On-demand Evolving Isolation Forest (OEIF) that adapts dynamically to online traffic shifts. These findings indicate that while Isolation Forest offers low computational time complexity $O(n\log n)$, raw iForest models require dimensionality reduction or dynamic parameter updating to maintain resilience against concept drift.

Recognizing that individual flow records do not capture the temporal dependencies inherent in multi-stage cyberattacks, researchers have extensively evaluated sequential deep learning models, particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and hybrid architectures (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Magar et al., 2025; Volpe et al., 2024). Volpe et al. (2024) evaluated an LSTM network paired with formal Petri Net control logic on the CSE-CIC-IDS2018 dataset, achieving a detection accuracy of 99.71% by modeling time-correlated packet sequences. To combine spatial feature extraction with temporal sequence learning, Magar et al. (2025) proposed a hybrid Random Forest and LSTM architecture on the CICIDS2017 benchmark dataset. After applying SMOTE for class balancing, the hybrid RF-LSTM model attained 99.90% accuracy, 99.97% precision, and a 0.9999 ROC-AUC score, outperforming both standalone Random Forest (99.88%) and standalone LSTM (99.38%). Similarly, Al Jallad et al. (2020) evaluated a hybrid Convolutional Neural Network and LSTM (CNN-LSTM) framework on NSL-KDD, achieving 98.39% overall accuracy and 100% precision on Denial of Service (DoS) attacks, outperforming standalone CNN (97.96%) and vanilla Recurrent Neural Networks (93.49%). Additionally, Santhosh Kumar et al. (2023) demonstrated that deep LSTM architectures reduced false-positive rates by 10% compared to Support Vector Machines, although standalone LSTMs exhibited lower classification accuracy on minority attack categories without specialized class balancing.

Synthesizing comparative results across offline empirical studies reveals distinct performance trade-offs, preferred evaluation metrics, and persistent methodological limitations (Ali et al., 2024; Maseer et al., 2021; Pinto Neto et al., 2025). Across major benchmark studies, evaluation metrics have expanded beyond simple classification accuracy to include precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, False Positive Rate (FPR), and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) to account for skewed class distributions (Ali et al., 2024; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). While supervised tree-based ensembles, such as Random Forest and XGBoost, achieve the highest overall classification accuracy and fastest inference speed on static tabular datasets, deep sequential architectures like LSTM and CNN-LSTM demonstrate superior capability in identifying temporal attack patterns and multi-stage exploits (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025).

Despite these analytical advancements, four major methodological limitations remain prevalent across static offline literature. First, a persistent lack of real-time latency and throughput metrics limits operational insight, as the vast majority of offline studies evaluate models strictly in batch mode, measuring isolated classification accuracy without quantifying end-to-end ingestion latency, streaming throughput, or memory consumption during live network socket transport (Ali et al., 2024; Pinto Neto et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Second, a simplified binary classification bias frequently inflates performance results, as many studies reduce intrusion detection to a binary problem of attack versus normal traffic, which masks complete model failure on rare minority classes such as User-to-Root (U2R) and Remote-to-Local (R2L) exploits (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Maseer et al., 2021; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). Third, high computational and training complexity creates a practical deployment barrier, given that complex deep neural networks and hybrid architectures require extensive training durations and high-performance GPU resources that exceed the capabilities of budget-constrained edge devices and real-time operational nodes (Ali et al., 2024; Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Finally, methodological data leakage risks distort reported accuracy, as improper preprocessing workflows that apply min-max scaling, global feature selection, or SMOTE oversampling across an entire dataset prior to train-test splitting introduce subtle leakage, yielding overly optimistic results that fail to generalize to uncurated production environments (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026).

### **2.4.3 Review of Real-Time and Resource-Constrained Deployments**

Real-time traffic processing and streaming inference represent a critical shift in network intrusion detection, transitioning security management from static batch evaluations to continuous, real-time threat analysis (Volpe et al., 2024; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). In streaming architectures, unbounded packet flows generated across transport sockets are ingested using message brokers such as Apache Kafka and routed to distributed stream processing engines like Apache Flink or Apache Spark Streaming (Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Empirical evaluations by Yahyaoui et al. (2021) demonstrate that true stream processing platforms like Apache Flink significantly outperform micro-batching architectures like Spark Streaming in packet throughput and ingestion latency, achieving processing speeds exceeding 196,000 packets per second when paired with feature reduction techniques. Furthermore, operationalizing continuous inference for sequential deep learning models requires structuring incoming packet streams into sliding temporal windows (Volpe et al., 2024). For example, Volpe et al. (2024) integrated a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network with a Petri Net decision engine, creating a virtual sensing architecture that analyzes consecutive packet sequences in real time and enforces automated flow control by allowing or blocking packet streams with a 91.5% attack blocking accuracy.

To bring real-time intrusion detection to small and medium businesses (SMBs) and edge networks, recent studies have investigated deployments on low-cost devices and resource-constrained hardware platforms (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). High-performance enterprise firewalls and cloud-based detection clusters often present prohibitive hardware and licensing costs, rendering them impractical for resource-inhibited environments (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). To address this deployability gap, Wijethilaka et al. (2025) developed a portable, cost-effective Network Intrusion Detection System (NIDS) deployed on a Raspberry Pi 4 single-board computer (\$35 to \$100 hardware cost). By evaluating machine learning algorithms including Random Forest, LSTM, XGBoost, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and Naive Bayes on the NF-UQ-NIDS benchmark dataset, their edge architecture achieved a 96.5% detection accuracy with an average response time of 50 milliseconds (Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Comparative hardware evaluations indicate that single-board devices such as the Raspberry Pi 4, Jetson Nano, and Odroid XU4 provide viable, low-latency processing capabilities for lightweight NIDS applications, bridging the gap between expensive commercial solutions (like Palo Alto or Cisco Firepower) and accessible local threat monitoring (Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

The operational effectiveness of real-time intrusion detection relies heavily on system-level metrics, including ingestion latency, packet throughput, memory requirements, and multi-tiered alert generation mechanisms (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Processing latency must remain strictly bounded to prevent packet queuing, buffer overflow, and dropped connections during high-velocity traffic spikes (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). When an inference engine identifies an anomalous or malicious traffic flow exceeding classification thresholds, the architecture triggers automated alert generation protocols to notify security personnel (Wijethilaka et al., 2025). In edge hardware deployments, alert generation is operationalized through a dual-layered notification system combining physical indicators and digital messaging (Wijethilaka et al., 2025). At the physical hardware layer, the system utilizes the General Purpose Input/Output (GPIO) pins of the single-board computer to drive visual status LEDs (green for benign conditions, red for detected threats) and activate physical buzzers for immediate on-site auditory alarms (Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Simultaneously, at the digital software layer, Python smtplib modules automatically generate and dispatch email alerts containing essential forensic metadata, such as source and destination IP addresses, timestamps, attack categories, and packet summaries, ensuring immediate remote situational awareness for administrators (Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

Despite promising experimental results, existing real-time and resource-constrained intrusion detection implementations face several persistent limitations and operational challenges (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Real-Time Malicious Traffic Detection, 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). A primary limitation centers on resource exhaustion threats, where volumetric traffic flooding (such as Distributed Denial of Service attacks) overwhelms the CPU processing capacity and memory bandwidth of low-cost edge devices, resulting in processing delays, buffer overflows, or missed threat detections (Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Additionally, streaming network data exhibits non-stationary distributions and concept drift, where evolving communication protocols and shifting traffic baselines cause static model performance to degrade over time (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Real-Time Malicious Traffic Detection, 2024). Overcoming concept drift requires adaptive online learning, dynamic feature selection, or administrator feedback loops, which introduce additional computational complexity (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Real-Time Malicious Traffic Detection, 2024). Finally, complex deep learning models and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) frameworks impose significant computational and memory overheads, creating a fundamental operational trade-off between model interpretability, zero-day detection precision, and real-time pipeline latency on edge hardware (Evaluating Machine Learning-Based Intrusion Detection Systems with Explainable AI, 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

## **2.5 User Interface and Visualization Architecture**

While the effectiveness of a network intrusion detection system depends heavily on the predictive accuracy and sub-second latency of its underlying machine learning models, its practical utility is ultimately defined by how effectively that threat intelligence is communicated to human operators. A robust visualization architecture bridges the gap between complex algorithmic outputs and actionable situational awareness, enabling security administrators to interpret high-velocity traffic streams and respond to anomalies without succumbing to alert fatigue. Accordingly, this section reviews the operational necessity of security dashboards, the technical challenges of rendering real-time streaming metrics, and the contemporary software frameworks that facilitate the deployment of interactive machine learning interfaces.

### **2.5.1 Role of Dashboards in Network Security Monitoring**

An Intrusion Detection System operating without an integrated visual user interface functions as a silent background algorithm, delivering analytical outputs that remain inaccessible to security operations personnel (Ali et al., 2024; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). In modern enterprise and industrial environments, security administrators must evaluate massive volumes of continuous network traffic, where high-speed streams can exceed tens of thousands of packet flow records per second (Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Human analysts cannot parse raw comma-separated value (CSV) logs, packet capture (PCAP) byte streams, or terminal console printouts at this velocity without experiencing severe cognitive overload and administrative delay (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Furthermore, high false-positive rates inherent in automated anomaly detectors contribute to alert fatigue, causing security teams to become desensitized to frequent warnings and potentially miss genuine threat incidents (Ali et al., 2024; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). Interactive visualization dashboards resolve these operational bottlenecks by serving as the primary mechanism for establishing situational awareness (Evaluating Machine Learning-Based Intrusion Detection Systems with Explainable AI, 2024; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). By translating high-dimensional feature vectors, anomaly confidence scores, and multi-class predictions into intuitive graphical intelligence, dashboards allow administrators to assess network health rapidly, verify alert validity, and execute timely incident response actions (Evaluating Machine Learning-Based Intrusion Detection Systems with Explainable AI, 2024; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023).

### **2.5.2 Real-Time Data Visualization and Alert Presentation**

Operationalizing real-time security monitoring introduces distinct software engineering challenges, particularly regarding the dynamic rendering of high-velocity streaming data (Khorasani et al., 2022; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Traditional data analysis relies on static, retrospective charting frameworks that require manual page refreshes or database re-queries to display updated records (Khorasani et al., 2022). In contrast, continuous threat detection demands dynamic, event-driven visualization frameworks capable of rendering data in motion (Khorasani et al., 2022; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). To ensure operational efficacy, the front-end visualization pipeline must match the sub-second processing latency of the underlying machine learning inference engine, which generates predictions within a 50 to 500-millisecond window (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Sub-second presentation latency ensures that critical security events, such as a sudden Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) traffic burst or a rapid port-scanning sequence, are rendered instantaneously on screen as visual alerts (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Effective alert presentation integrates multi-panel telemetry displays, combining real-time gauge metrics for total throughput, historical line charts for temporal anomaly scores, pie distributions comparing benign versus malicious class ratios, and tabular alert feeds populated with critical forensic metadata, such as source IP addresses, destination ports, and threat classifications (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021).

### **2.5.3 Dashboard Frameworks for Machine Learning Applications**

Selecting an appropriate dashboard deployment framework requires evaluating performance trade-offs between heavy enterprise monitoring stacks, traditional multi-tier web architectures, and modern lightweight Python frameworks (Khorasani et al., 2022; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Heavy commercial security information and event management (SIEM) platforms and traditional stack combinations (such as Elasticsearch, Logstash, and Kibana, known as the ELK stack) provide robust enterprise log indexing but impose high resource overhead, complex setup procedures, and steep licensing costs that are unfeasible for small businesses or resource-constrained edge networks (Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023; Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). Similarly, building custom web dashboards using conventional full-stack Python frameworks like Django or Flask requires managing complex front-end web layers, including HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and asynchronous WebSocket protocols, creating significant engineering friction (Khorasani et al., 2022). To streamline deployment, modern machine learning applications increasingly utilize Streamlit, an open-source, pure-Python web framework designed specifically for rapid data application development (Khorasani et al., 2022). Streamlit converts standard Python scripts into interactive, responsive web interfaces without requiring front-end web development expertise, executing both data processing and UI logic within a unified Python codebase (Khorasani et al., 2022). Its reactive execution architecture, session state management, and native compatibility with visualization libraries (such as Matplotlib, Plotly, and Pandas) allow developers to bind live machine learning streaming pipelines directly to interactive web dashboards with minimal computational overhead (Khorasani et al., 2022).

### **2.5.4 Review of Related Monitoring and Visualization Systems**

A critical review of empirical intrusion detection literature reveals a pervasive methodological gap: the majority of published studies focus exclusively on model training and static accuracy evaluation, neglecting the development of functional, user-facing visualization interfaces (Maseer et al., 2021; Volpe et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Numerous benchmark evaluations report near-perfect classification metrics on static flow datasets but output prediction results solely to command-line interfaces, offline text logs, or confusion matrices (Maseer et al., 2021; Santhosh Kumar et al., 2023). Where alerting mechanisms are implemented in real-time edge studies, such as the Raspberry Pi deployment by Wijethilaka et al. (2025), notification relies on basic terminal output, automated email dispatches via Python smtplib modules, or physical hardware indicators like local LEDs and buzzers, omitting an interactive graphical user interface. While enterprise-grade distributed streaming architectures utilize specialized big data tools like Apache Flink paired with Grafana dashboards for storage and visualization (Yahyaoui et al., 2021), these multi-node environments require substantial computational infrastructure and specialized operational management. Consequently, the lack of an integrated, lightweight, low-cost graphical monitoring dashboard in current academic Network Intrusion Detection System (NIDS) literature represents a clear practical limitation. Developing an end-to-end NIDS that combines streaming machine learning inference with an accessible Streamlit visualization interface directly addresses this deployability gap, transitioning theoretical detection models into a complete, operational software application (Khorasani et al., 2022; Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021).

## **2.6 Summary of Literature and Identified Research Gaps**

A comprehensive review of the literature demonstrates a clear evolutionary trajectory in network security, transitioning from rigid, signature-based defenses toward adaptive, behavioral machine learning paradigms. The empirical evidence confirms that supervised models like Random Forest, unsupervised models like Isolation Forest, and sequential deep learning architectures like LSTM are highly capable of isolating complex, zero-day threat patterns. Furthermore, the progression of benchmark datasets from the outdated KDD Cup 99 to the contemporary, well-balanced IDS2025 dataset has provided researchers with the high-quality flow features necessary to train these models without introducing severe class imbalance or frequency bias.

### **2.6.1 Synthesis of Reviewed Studies**

The empirical literature on machine learning-based Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) demonstrates a progressive evolution across algorithmic paradigms, benchmark datasets, and real-time deployment methodologies (Ali et al., 2024; Maseer et al., 2021; E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). Supervised tree-based ensemble models, particularly Random Forest, consistently achieve superior classification accuracy, high precision, and fast inference speeds on structured tabular network flow features (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026). These tree-based models excel at capturing complex non-linear feature interactions and mitigating prediction variance when evaluated on benchmark datasets like NSL-KDD, CICIDS2017, and NF-UQ-NIDS (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Maseer et al., 2021; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). However, supervised tree-based algorithms remain fundamentally limited by their reliance on historical human-annotated attack labels and struggle to model temporal relationships across consecutive network flows (Hamidou & Mehdi, 2025; Magar et al., 2025; Volpe et al., 2024).

To address the challenge of unannotated traffic streams and zero-day threat detection, unsupervised anomaly isolation models like Isolation Forest operate without pre-existing attack labels by recursively partitioning feature space to isolate rare, structural outliers from benign communication baselines (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Lua et al., 2024). While Isolation Forest offers linear time complexity and fast execution, raw implementations are sensitive to high-dimensional noise and feature drift, often requiring hyperparameter optimization or dynamic feature selection to maintain stability (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Real-Time Malicious Traffic Detection, 2024). Conversely, sequential deep learning architectures, specifically Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and hybrid models like CNN-LSTM or RF-LSTM, resolve the temporal limitations of static classifiers by incorporating gated memory units that track time-series dependencies across consecutive flows (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Magar et al., 2025; Volpe et al., 2024). These sequential models demonstrate high efficacy in identifying multi-stage, distributed attack vectors such as Denial of Service floods and port scans (Al Jallad et al., 2020; Volpe et al., 2024).

In real-time deployment literature, researchers have transitioned toward evaluating continuous traffic processing across distributed stream engines like Apache Flink and low-cost hardware platforms such as the Raspberry Pi 4 (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). These deployments demonstrate that lightweight edge systems can achieve real-time threat classification with sub-second response times (Wijethilaka et al., 2025). However, a comparative synthesis indicates that while supervised ensembles deliver optimal tabular accuracy, unsupervised isolation provides zero-day resilience, and deep recurrent networks capture temporal sequences, existing studies rarely integrate all three paradigms within a unified real-time pipeline (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Magar et al., 2025; Volpe et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

### **2.6.2 Identified Research Gaps**

A critical synthesis of the reviewed literature reveals four methodological and practical gaps that persist across current intrusion detection studies.

The first is a real-time pipeline gap. The vast majority of published studies evaluate models on static, offline datasets, where pre-collected records are processed in batch mode (Jedh et al., 2022). These studies often report near-perfect accuracy, but they rarely demonstrate continuous traffic ingestion, live feature extraction, and real-time inference over a socket in a dynamic operational environment.

The second is a latency and throughput documentation gap. Computational efficiency is frequently omitted or under-reported. Most studies report accuracy, precision, and recall, but few measure or document end-to-end processing latency (milliseconds per flow) and system throughput (flows processed per second). Both are needed to show that sub-second detection can be sustained without dropped records during high-velocity traffic spikes.

The third is a comparative evaluation gap. Existing literature favors single-algorithm proposals, or evaluates algorithms under inconsistent experimental settings, with varying preprocessing pipelines and mismatched feature subsets (Maseer et al., 2021). Few studies benchmark supervised tree-based ensembles (Random Forest), unsupervised anomaly isolation (Isolation Forest), and sequential deep learning (LSTM) under identical experimental conditions, using both predictive accuracy and system performance metrics.

The fourth is a monitoring and visualization gap. Most academic intrusion detection frameworks output predictions only to command-line interfaces, terminal logs, or static confusion matrices (Maseer et al., 2021; Santhosh Kumar, 2023). Even deployable edge systems often limit alerting to terminal text, email notifications sent through the smtplib module, or physical LEDs and buzzers (Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Integrated, lightweight, low-cost interactive dashboards that present real-time threat telemetry, live stream metrics, and actionable alert interpretations for security personnel remain rare (Khorasani et al., 2022).

### 2.6.3 Relevance of the Identified Gaps to the Proposed System

The proposed Network Intrusion Detection System directly addresses each of the identified research gaps through an end-to-end software engineering architecture (Khorasani et al., 2022; Volpe et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). To bridge the real-time pipeline gap, the system establishes a continuous streaming inference pipeline capable of capturing, normalizing, and classifying live network socket traffic dynamically (Volpe et al., 2024; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). To resolve the comparative evaluation gap, the proposed framework systematically compares three distinct mathematical paradigms (Random Forest, Isolation Forest, and LSTM) under identical data preprocessing workflows and feature subsets, evaluating their performance across both spatial tabular and temporal sequence representations (Elsaid & Binbusayyis, 2024; Magar et al., 2025; Suresh Kumar et al., 2026).

To eliminate the latency and throughput documentation gap, the evaluation methodology incorporates rigorous system-level profiling alongside standard predictive metrics (Wijethilaka et al., 2025; Yahyaoui et al., 2021). The proposed system evaluates predictive performance using Precision, Recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC metrics to account for class imbalance, while simultaneously measuring pipeline latency (milliseconds) and system throughput (packets per second) under streaming replay conditions (E. C. P. Neto et al., 2024; Wijethilaka et al., 2025). Finally, to close the monitoring and visualization gap, the system integrates a live, lightweight web dashboard developed using the Streamlit framework (Khorasani et al., 2022). This dashboard provides interactive, sub-second visualization of live traffic statistics, temporal threat distribution charts, multi-class predictions, and detailed forensic alert logs, transforming complex backend algorithmic outputs into an operational cybersecurity product (Khorasani et al., 2022; Wijethilaka et al., 2025).

## **3.2 SYSTEM ANALYSIS**

System analysis establishes what the system has to do and whether it can be built with the resources available. It begins with the problem that the system addresses, then states the requirements, tests their feasibility, and ends with an analysis of the data on which the system depends.

### **3.2.1 Problem Definition**

The existing practice that this work responds to is offline evaluation of machine learning intrusion detectors. In a typical offline workflow, a labelled dataset is loaded entirely into memory, split into training and test partitions, used to train a model, and scored on the held-out rows, and the results are reported as precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC. This workflow is valuable for comparing the detection quality of models, but it leaves several questions about real-time behaviour unanswered.

First, an offline file has no arrival process. All records are available at once, so there is no emission rate, no queueing, and no backlog, and the time dimension is absent. Second, where latency is reported at all, it is usually the isolated time taken by the model to score a record. It excludes the other steps that a deployed system must perform for every record, namely reading it, transporting it, parsing it, scaling its features, serialising the result, storing it, and displaying it. Third, offline scoring is normally vectorised over large matrices, so the cost per record when records are handled one at a time can differ greatly from the cost implied by a batch figure. This difference is most pronounced for sequence models such as the LSTM, which must also maintain a window of recent records. Fourth, offline experiments often fit the feature scaler on the whole dataset, whereas a live system must apply a scaler that was fitted at training time to each record on its own. Finally, an offline experiment produces tables of numbers, and nobody can observe the system making decisions as traffic arrives.

The problem addressed in this study can therefore be stated as follows. There is no integrated, reproducible pipeline, small enough to run on a single machine, that allows Isolation Forest, Random Forest, and LSTM detectors to be compared on the IDS2025 dataset with respect to both detection quality and end-to-end real-time performance, from the moment a record is emitted to the moment its result is available on a monitoring dashboard.

To solve this problem, the system must satisfy the following obligations. It must replay labelled flow records as a timed stream at a configurable rate. It must classify each record as it arrives, using any of the three models under identical preprocessing. It must measure the end-to-end latency of every record and the sustained throughput of the pipeline. It must present the results to a network administrator on a live dashboard. It must log every run together with its configuration and test environment so that the results can be reproduced. In formal terms, the detector is a function f that maps an input record x in the input space X to a label in the output space Y, and it is acceptable only if the time t(f(x)) it takes to produce the label does not exceed the interval Δt at which records arrive, as expressed in Equation 3.1.

### **3.2.4 Dataset Analysis: IDS2025**

The system is built and evaluated on IDS2025, a dataset published on Mendeley Data in November 2025 (Panigrahi & Borah, 2025). It is a refined version of the CICIDS2017 dataset (Sharafaldin et al., 2018), in which minority attack classes were relabelled and merged, redundant records were removed or resampled, inconsistent labels were corrected, and attack records were spread more evenly across the daily files. Each record describes one network flow with about 80 features extracted by CICFlowMeter. The data are organised as daily CSV files from Monday to Friday. Because the dataset is recent, no independent benchmarking study based on it had been found at the time of writing, and this is one of the reasons for choosing it: results on it are not yet saturated by prior work.

The features are statistical summaries of a flow and fall into a small number of groups: flow duration, packet counts and packet length statistics in the forward and backward directions, inter-arrival times, TCP flag counts, header lengths, rates in bytes and packets per second, window sizes, and active and idle times. The protocol field identifies the transport protocol and is used for the protocol mix on the dashboard. The dataset description does not state whether flow identifiers, addresses, ports, or timestamps are retained. The design therefore assumes that any such identifier columns that are present will be removed before training so that the models cannot learn from them, and that a timestamp, if present, is used only to order the replay.

The label column assigns every record to one of seven classes, which are described in Table 3.6. For the primary comparison, the classes are collapsed into a binary label (Normal or Malicious), because this is the decision an alerting system must make. The Random Forest and the LSTM are additionally trained and reported on the seven-class label to show which attack types are detected well.

**Table 3.6: Classes in the IDS2025 dataset and how they are handled**

| **Class**    | **Description**                                                             | **Handling in this study**                               |
|--------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| Normal       | Benign background traffic                                                   | Negative class; sole training class for Isolation Forest |
| Botnet       | Traffic from compromised hosts communicating with a controller              | Positive class; stratified split                         |
| Brute Force  | Repeated login attempts, for example on FTP or SSH services                 | Positive class; stratified split                         |
| DoS / DDoS   | Denial-of-service attacks of several types, merged into one class           | Positive class; stratified split                         |
| Infiltration | An internal host compromised and used to reach other hosts                  | Positive class; rare class, class weights applied        |
| PortScan     | Probing of hosts for open ports                                             | Positive class; stratified split                         |
| Web Attack   | Attacks on web applications, for example injection and cross-site scripting | Positive class; stratified split                         |

*Source: class list from the IDS2025 dataset description (Panigrahi & Borah, 2025). Per-class record counts are not published in the description and are computed on loading, then reported in Chapter Four.*

The class distribution deserves a direct statement. The dataset description says that class imbalance was reduced by merging and resampling, but it does not publish record counts per class, so the counts will be computed when the data are loaded and reported in Chapter Four. Even after rebalancing it is likely that benign records and the largest attack classes outnumber the rarest ones. The design therefore does not depend on a balanced dataset. Stratified splitting preserves the class proportions in every partition, class weights are used when training the supervised models, and accuracy is not used as a primary metric because it hides poor performance on small classes. Precision, recall, and F1-score, together with ROC-AUC, are used instead.

Figure 3.3 shows the data preparation pipeline. The daily files are loaded and merged into one table. Exact duplicate rows are removed. Infinite values, which arise in the rate features when a flow has zero duration, are replaced by missing values, and rows that still contain missing values are dropped when they form less than one percent of the data, and are otherwise filled with training-set medians. Columns that are constant, and identifier columns, are dropped. Highly correlated feature pairs (absolute correlation above 0.98) are reviewed and one column of each pair is removed. Labels are encoded into the binary and seven-class forms and saved in a label map.

The data are then split in a stratified manner into a training partition (70 percent), a validation partition (15 percent), and a test partition (15 percent), using a fixed random seed of 42 and the seven-class label as the stratification variable. The training partition is used to fit the scaler and the models. The validation partition is used to choose hyperparameters, to stop training early, and to set decision thresholds. The test partition is touched only for the final evaluation and is also the source of the stream replayed through the system. Because the split is made before any scaling, no statistic of the validation or test partition influences the scaler.

Scaling uses standardisation, in which each feature x is transformed to (x − μ) / σ, where μ and σ are the mean and standard deviation of that feature in the training partition. Features with a strongly skewed, non-negative distribution are first transformed with log(1 + x). The fitted scaler and the list of retained features are saved to disk and loaded by the inference engine, so that the same transformation is applied offline and online. The Random Forest does not need scaling, but it uses the same pipeline so that all three detectors present one interface to the engine.

[figure]

**Figure 3.3: Data preparation pipeline for the IDS2025 dataset**

Two limitations of this procedure are acknowledged. First, a random stratified split places flows that are close neighbours in time, for example many flows of the same port scan, in both the training and test partitions, which can make results look better than they would be on a completely new capture. Removing duplicates reduces the effect, and if time allows, a robustness check will train on the earlier days and test on a later day. Second, the stream sends records of the test partition in their original file order. The Isolation Forest and Random Forest treat each record independently and are unaffected by this order, whereas the LSTM relies on it, a point that is treated in Section 3.3.10.

## **3.3 SYSTEM DESIGN**

System design describes how the requirements of Section 3.2 are met. The design is presented from the outside in: first the overall architecture, then the flow of data, the data model, the interaction between the user and the system, the structure of the code, the behaviour over time, the interface, the modules, the database, and finally the design of the machine learning models and of the evaluation.

### **3.3.1 System Architecture**

The system follows a layered pipeline architecture, shown in Figure 3.4. Each layer has one responsibility and communicates only with its neighbours, so a layer can be changed or measured without disturbing the others. The data layer holds the IDS2025 file and the streamer that reads it. The transport layer is a local TCP socket that carries one JSON message per record. The processing layer contains the inference engine, which is expanded in the figure to show its internal steps. The storage and monitoring layer holds the SQLite database and the latency logger. The presentation layer contains the Streamlit dashboard and its controls.

[figure]

**Figure 3.4: Layered architecture of the real-time NIDS with the inference engine expanded**

Data move through the system in four numbered flows, which correspond to the numbers in Figure 3.4. In flow 1, the streamer reads the next record from the IDS2025 file, stamps the emission time t<sub>0</sub>, serialises the record as JSON, and sends it over the socket at the configured interval Δt. In flow 2, the inference engine receives the message, validates and parses it, scales its features with the saved scaler, passes the scaled vector to the selected detector, and builds a result containing the label, the score, and the timestamps. In flow 3, the result is handed to the latency logger, which writes it to the database and records the final timestamp t<sub>4</sub> when the write is committed. In flow 4, the dashboard reads the latest rows from the database at a fixed refresh interval and renders the charts, the alert table, and the latency panel. Control actions such as selecting a model, changing the emission interval, and starting or stopping the stream travel in the opposite direction, from the dashboard to the streamer and the engine, over a separate control channel so that they do not add to the measured latency of the data path.

Four design decisions follow from the requirements. First, the producer and consumer are separate processes joined by a socket, and not two functions in one program, so that the cost of serialisation and transport is included in the measured latency, which is the quantity that offline studies leave out. Second, all three models sit behind one detector interface, so the engine is identical for every model and any difference in latency is attributable to the model. Third, timestamps are taken at the boundaries between components, which allows the total latency to be broken down into its parts. Fourth, a message broker such as Kafka was not adopted. For a single machine and a single stream it would add installation effort and a source of timing variability without improving the validity of the comparison, and it would make the study harder to reproduce.

The tables are normalised to the third normal form (Codd, 1970). All fields hold single values, which satisfies the first normal form; the hyperparameters and library versions are stored as one JSON text value each because they are only ever read as a whole. Every table has a single-column primary key, so no field can depend on part of a key, which satisfies the second normal form. The third normal form is met by removing transitive dependencies. The hardware description depends on the environment and not on the run, so it is held in TEST_ENVIRONMENT. The description of a flow depends on the flow and not on the prediction, so it is held in FLOW_RECORD and is not repeated for every prediction. The latency L is a derived quantity and is therefore not stored. It is computed from the timestamps by a database view, so it cannot become inconsistent with them.

Two indexes support the dashboard queries: one on the run and emission time of predictions, to fetch the latest rows of a run, and a unique index on the row index of flow records. The primary and foreign keys implement the relationships of Figure 3.8, and foreign key enforcement is switched on for every connection. All statements use bound parameters and never string concatenation, as required by NFR6. Writing one row per record and committing it before stamping t<sub>4</sub> may make storage a significant part of the latency. This is an intended part of the measurement. If it proves to dominate, the commit strategy can be changed and the effect reported, but the definition of t<sub>4</sub> will not change.

The model is trained without labels on the normal records of the training partition. Its hyperparameters are chosen by grid search on the validation partition, using F1-score and ROC-AUC. A decision threshold τ on the score is then chosen on the validation partition to maximise the F1-score. This calibration uses the validation labels, so the model is better described as unsupervised in training with a supervised threshold. The ROC-AUC is computed directly from the scores and does not depend on τ.

#### ***Random Forest***

The Random Forest is an ensemble of decision trees, each trained on a bootstrap sample with random feature subsets, whose votes are combined (Breiman, 2001). It is trained on all training records. Hyperparameters are chosen by randomised search with stratified five-fold cross-validation inside the training partition, scored by the F1-score for the binary task and the macro F1-score for the seven-class task. The best configuration is refitted on the full training partition and checked on the validation partition. The cost of one prediction grows with the number of trees and their depth, which is why these two hyperparameters matter for latency as well as for accuracy.

#### ***Long Short-Term Memory network***

The LSTM is a recurrent network with gated memory cells that can learn dependencies across a sequence (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997). Its input is a window of the W most recent scaled records, so the decision for a record can use the records that preceded it. The network has one or two LSTM layers followed by dropout and a dense output layer, and is trained with the Adam optimiser and class-weighted cross-entropy loss. Training stops early when the validation loss has not improved for five epochs. Training windows are built separately within each partition, so that no window crosses a partition boundary.

At run time the LSTMDetector keeps a buffer of the last W records received and predicts on each arrival. The first W − 1 records of a run have no full window, so they are marked as warm-up and are excluded from the detection metrics. The windowing design rests on one assumption that must be checked when the data are loaded: that the order of rows in the IDS2025 files preserves capture order, so that neighbouring rows are related flows. Because the split of Section 3.2.4 is random and stratified, a window in the test partition contains records that were neighbours within that partition, which is a weaker context than neighbours in the original capture. If inspection shows that the file order carries no sequential meaning, or if the windows give no benefit over the single-record models, the fallback is to treat the F features of a single flow as a sequence of F steps, a formulation commonly used for tabular flow data, which removes the dependence on record order. Whichever formulation is used will be stated in Chapter Four.

#### ***Random seeds, training procedure, and leakage controls***

The data split uses a fixed seed of 42. For each model, the final training and evaluation are repeated with five seeds (42 to 46), and results are reported as the mean and standard deviation, so that differences between models can be judged against run-to-run variation. The seed of every run is stored in MODEL_RUN. During inference tests each model is restricted to a single processor thread, so that parallelism does not hide the cost per record, while training may use all cores.

Data leakage, in which information from the evaluation data influences training, is the commonest cause of over-optimistic results in intrusion detection. Table 3.12 lists the controls applied.

For each run, the median, 95th percentile, 99th percentile, mean, and maximum of L are reported, together with the mean of each component t<sub>tx</sub>, t<sub>pre</sub>, t<sub>inf</sub>, and t<sub>ser</sub>, so that the share of the model in the total latency is visible. Throughput, defined in Equation 3.7, is the number of records processed per second over the duration of a run. The maximum sustainable throughput of each model is found by reducing Δt step by step (100, 50, 20, 10, 5, and 1 ms) until the 95th percentile of L exceeds Δt or the backlog of unprocessed records grows.

#### ***Test conditions***

Two load conditions are used. In the steady condition, records are emitted at a constant interval Δt. In the burst condition, the stream is idle at the base rate and then a block of 1,000 records is emitted back to back, which tests how the system copes with a sudden surge and how quickly it clears the resulting backlog. Each scenario is run for every model, and each combination is repeated five times. Steady runs use a sample of 5,000 records and burst runs a sample of 2,000, drawn from the test partition with a fixed seed and replayed in their original order. The first 100 records of every run are treated as warm-up and excluded from latency statistics. Table 3.13 lists the scenarios.

**Table 3.13: Test scenarios for the system evaluation**

| **ID** | **Condition**       | **Emission pattern**                       | **Purpose**                            | **Main metrics**                        |
|--------|---------------------|--------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| S1     | Steady, baseline    | Δt = 100 ms (10 records/s)                 | Check the latency target of NFR1       | Median, p95, p99 of L                   |
| S2     | Steady, high rate   | Δt = 10 ms (100 records/s)                 | Check the throughput target of NFR2    | L, throughput, backlog                  |
| S3     | Steady, rate search | Δt reduced from 100 ms to 1 ms             | Find the maximum sustainable rate      | Throughput at which p95 of L exceeds Δt |
| S4     | Burst               | 1,000 records at once after an idle period | Measure peak latency and recovery time | Maximum L, time to clear backlog        |

As a correctness check, the labels predicted for the streamed sample are compared with the labels predicted offline for the same records. They must agree, which shows that the streaming pipeline does not alter the behaviour of the models.

The evaluation has limits that are stated openly. The stream is a replay of a recorded dataset and not live traffic, the tests run on one machine, and the results describe that machine and that dataset. They allow a fair comparison of the three models under identical conditions, but they do not prove performance on other networks or hardware.

## **3.4 SUMMARY**

This chapter presented the methodology and the analysis and design of the real-time NIDS. The study follows the Design Science Research Methodology as an iterative cycle, with CRISP-DM nested inside the design and development activity, and it rejects the Waterfall model because the main unknowns can only be resolved by building and measuring. The analysis defined the problem as the lack of an integrated pipeline that compares three models on both detection quality and end-to-end latency, stated fifteen functional and eight non-functional requirements, showed that the project is technically, economically, and operationally feasible, and described the IDS2025 dataset together with its cleaning, scaling, and stratified split.

The design described a layered architecture in which an inference engine sits behind a common detector interface, data flow diagrams at three levels, an entity-relationship diagram and a normalised SQLite database, use case, class, and sequence diagrams that define the timestamps of the latency, a Streamlit dashboard wireframe, and a module structure. The machine learning design specified the input, hyperparameter ranges, training procedure, random seeds, and leakage controls of each model. The evaluation design defined the detection metrics, the latency and throughput measures, the steady and burst conditions, and the recording of the test hardware.

Chapter Four presents the implementation of the system according to this design, the implementation issues encountered, particularly those concerning performance and real-time behaviour, and the unit, integration, system, and user acceptance testing, together with the results of the evaluation.
